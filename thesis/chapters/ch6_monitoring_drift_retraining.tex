\chapter{Monitoring, Drift Detection, and Retraining}
\label{chap:monitoring}

This chapter focuses on monitoring, drift logic, trigger policy, and retraining workflow as implemented in the current stage-6 and stage-7 runtime path. Unless explicitly marked as an experimental branch, the term \emph{runtime path} refers to the defended lightweight monitoring and governance implementation submitted with the thesis. The calibrated MMD experiments described later in this chapter and in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} are treated as branch evidence for drift auditing and alerting, not as the final defended retraining trigger.

\section{Three-Layer Post-Inference Monitoring}

Before describing the three signal groups, this section fixes the vocabulary used throughout the chapter. \emph{Confidence} refers to the softmax probability assigned to the predicted class. It is informative only to the extent that the model is calibrated, and modern deep networks are well known to report overconfident probabilities without temperature scaling or comparable corrections \cite{guo2017calibration}. \emph{Uncertainty} refers to prediction ambiguity and instability; the defended runtime records lightweight softmax-derived proxies, while Monte Carlo dropout and related Bayesian approximations remain relevant research extensions \cite{gal2016dropout}. Recent theoretical work also cautions that two models can achieve identical expected calibration error and still behave very differently under shift, so calibration metrics are not by themselves a substitute for governance \cite{blasiok2024calibration}. \emph{Drift} refers to changes in the effective input distribution relative to the training reference, a condition repeatedly documented in wearable HAR under user variability, placement changes, and preprocessing mismatch \cite{dhekane2024transfer,wang2024enhancing,akbari2020personalizing}.

Stage 6 evaluates model behavior using three signal groups:

\begin{itemize}
	\item confidence and uncertainty indicators,
	\item temporal-transition stability indicators,
	\item drift indicators derived from baseline comparisons.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch6_monitoring_governance} gives a compact view of the complete post-inference governance chain from Stage~5 metrics through Stage~6 signal assessment to the Stage~7 action decision.

The monitoring layer is intentionally warning-oriented. In the final submitted defaults, Stage~6 warns at 0.70 mean confidence (\texttt{confidence\_warn\_threshold}), 20\% uncertain windows (\texttt{uncertain\_pct\_threshold}), 35\% transition rate, 1.8 drift z-score, and 85\% dominant-class share, with a 95\% dominant-class critical band. These values are conservative operating thresholds for this repository, not clinical limits, not statistical guarantees, and not universal cutoffs for wearable HAR. Their purpose is to surface sessions that deserve review before any model-mutation decision is considered.

Before Stage~6 consumes those signals, the preceding Stage~4--5 contract now records the evidence needed to make collapse analysis auditable. Stage~4 inference is owned by \path{src/components/model_inference.py}, which persists the prediction CSV, class-index NPY, probability NPY, metadata JSON, and \path{inference_report.json}. Stage~5 evaluation is owned by \path{src/components/model_evaluation.py} and handles unlabeled production batches by reporting distribution, confidence, entropy, top-two margin, and dominant-class concentration without fabricating accuracy. The verification run \texttt{20260502\_224950} produced 1814 predictions with probability tensor shape $(1814,11)$, confirmed that saved class indices match the CSV predictions, and surfaced \texttt{hand\_tapping} dominance at 100\% with mean confidence 0.998. This result is therefore treated as an operational collapse signal entering monitoring, not as an artifact-writing failure or label-mapping error.

\section{Trigger Policy in Stage 7}

Stage 7 converts monitoring signals into one governance action:
\texttt{none}, \texttt{monitor}, \texttt{review}, \texttt{queue\_retrain}, or \texttt{trigger}.

The default trigger thresholds are governance-oriented rather than warning-oriented, including confidence warning at 0.65 and critical at 0.50. The apparent inversion is deliberate: Stage~6 can raise an early monitoring warning at 0.70, while Stage~7 escalates only when the wider policy context justifies action. This split is consistent with MLOps literature that treats runtime trigger rules as a decision layer separate from raw model telemetry \cite{amershi2019mlops,kreuzberger2023mlops}. In health-adjacent settings, that separation is load-bearing, because signals that look anomalous at the instrument level must still be evaluated under behavioral plausibility and evidence quality before being used to change the model \cite{khattak2023mlhops}.

Decision aggregation follows a voting-style policy over confidence, temporal, and drift status, with escalation and cooldown guards.

\section{Collapse Guard and Cooldown Semantics}

A collapse guard is evaluated before any model-mutation decision. Collapse-like patterns (for example, very high dominant-class concentration) are routed to \texttt{review} with \texttt{should\_retrain=false}. This conservative behavior avoids unsafe retraining from unlabeled degenerate outputs.

An alternative design would apply test-time adaptation strategies such as AdaBN-style batch-statistics updates or entropy-minimization based TENT to reshape the active model toward the incoming distribution without labels \cite{li2016adabn,wang2021tent}. This thesis deliberately does not take that route. Test-time adaptation mutates the operational model in a way that is difficult to audit in an offline, file-based run, and may amplify the same collapse behavior that the guard is designed to detect. The collapse guard is therefore a governance-first response rather than an adaptation-first one.

Cooldown is then applied as a final gate to prevent rapid retraining loops. If cooldown is active, decisions are downgraded to queued behavior until the cooldown window expires.

\section{Retraining and Baseline Governance}

Stages 8--10 are optional and gated:

\begin{itemize}
	\item stage 8 retraining runs only when governance permits,
	\item stage 9 handles registration and optional promotion,
	\item stage 10 updates monitoring baselines, with shared-baseline promotion controlled by explicit flags.
\end{itemize}

This design keeps model updates controlled, auditable, and reversible. Transfer-learning and unsupervised domain-adaptation work in inertial-sensor HAR motivates this caution, since naive full retraining on new wearable batches can degrade previously learned behavior when labels are scarce or partially shifted \cite{dhekane2024transfer,chakma2023imuda}. Continual-learning strategies such as Elastic Weight Consolidation formalize the same intuition by constraining updates to parameters that were important for earlier tasks \cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc}. The retraining gate here does not implement a specific continual-learning objective, but its net effect is aligned with that caution: retraining is only permitted when multiple governance signals justify mutating the active runtime model.

\section{Scope of the Governance Claim}

The monitoring and trigger logic described in this chapter is a concrete realization of the broader \emph{MLHOps} view that machine-learning systems in health-adjacent settings need governance layers that are explicit, auditable, and separable from the model itself \cite{khattak2023mlhops}. The contribution of this chapter is not a new detector or a new retraining rule. It is the claim that these components can be composed into an offline-first system where retraining remains a deliberated action rather than an automatic reflex of monitoring telemetry.

The distinction between monitoring severity and retraining decision is not a design assumption but a verifiable property of the defended artifact evidence. Both final Stage~1--7 evidence runs confirm the same governance pattern. The Garmin unlabeled run (\texttt{20260508\_235323}, 1{,}304 windows) and the User~7 labeled run (\texttt{20260508\_235356}, 989~windows) both record \texttt{alert\_level=CRITICAL} in their monitoring output alongside \texttt{should\_retrain=false} and \texttt{action=review} in their trigger decision files. These are separate output fields with separate meanings, and their joint occurrence is not a contradiction.

\texttt{alert\_level} encodes monitoring severity: \texttt{CRITICAL} indicates that mean confidence fell below the warning threshold and the uncertain-window proportion exceeded its configured limit. \texttt{should\_retrain} and \texttt{action} encode the governance outcome: given \texttt{auto\_retrain=false}, no labeled retraining pool available, and no resolved collapse condition, Stage~7 correctly routes both sessions to human review rather than authorising automatic model mutation. Both runs also report \texttt{drift\_status=HEALTHY} and \texttt{temporal\_status=HEALTHY}, confirming that the input distribution remained within the training baseline even while confidence signals were in the critical band.

This separation is the core governance claim of the chapter. A monitoring system that cannot express high operational concern without immediately authorising a model mutation cannot be governed. \texttt{auto\_retrain=false} is not a placeholder for future work or a missing feature; it is the deliberate policy for a proof-of-concept runtime operating on a single external participant without a labeled retraining pool. Automatic retraining under these conditions would substitute monitoring artifacts for evidence, a failure mode documented across operational ML pipeline reviews~\cite{paleyes2022challenges,khattak2023mlhops}.

\section{Evaluation Metrics: Labeled and Unlabeled Conditions}
\label{sec:eval_metrics}

The pipeline uses two distinct families of evaluation metrics depending on whether ground-truth labels are available at the time of evaluation. Labeled metrics are used during training-time validation and when evaluating against the held-out test set; they measure classification performance directly. Unlabeled or proxy metrics are used during Stage~6 post-inference monitoring on production-style batches; they measure observable properties of the model's output distribution that are informative about operational risk without requiring ground truth. Understanding both families, and the relationship between them, is essential for interpreting the experimental results in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}.

\subsection{Labeled Evaluation Metrics}

Accuracy measures the fraction of windows for which the predicted class matches the ground-truth label. While accuracy is the most commonly reported metric in wearable HAR literature, it is insufficient as a standalone measure for the eleven-class activity recognition problem addressed in this thesis. A model that predicts only the dominant class for every input can achieve high accuracy if the class distribution is skewed, without demonstrating any discriminative ability across the full activity taxonomy \cite{bulling2014tutorial,lara2013survey,hammerla2016deep}. The hand-tapping dominance behavior observed in the preprocessing ablation --- in which a single configuration caused 96.72\% of windows to be predicted as one class --- would produce a nominally high accuracy on any dataset in which hand-tapping is frequently labeled, even though the model is operationally useless for recognizing any other activity. Accuracy must therefore be interpreted alongside class-level metrics rather than in isolation.

Macro-averaged F1 score addresses this limitation by computing the harmonic mean of precision and recall separately for each class and then averaging those per-class scores with equal weight across all eleven classes, regardless of the number of samples in each class. This weighting scheme is chosen deliberately: it penalizes the collapse behavior directly, because a model that concentrates all predictions on one class achieves zero F1 for the ten remaining classes, dragging the macro average toward zero even when single-class accuracy is high \cite{bulling2014tutorial}. The choice of macro over weighted averaging reflects the thesis's objective of maintaining recognition capability across the full set of anxiety-related activities, not merely optimizing the population-level metric.

Per-class precision and recall provide the diagnostic granularity that macro F1 summarises. In the context of the collapse behavior documented in this thesis, per-class metrics reveal which specific activity classes bear the cost of prediction concentration: high hand-tapping precision paired with near-zero recall on nail biting, hair pulling, and similar classes is the signature of collapse. The confusion matrix complements these scalar metrics by visualizing the full pattern of misclassification: a collapsed model produces a confusion matrix in which one column is densely populated while others are sparse, a structure that is immediately distinguishable from the dispersed misclassification pattern of a well-functioning classifier.

\subsection{Unlabeled and Proxy Evaluation Metrics}

Ground-truth labels are unavailable during production-style inference on wearable batches collected outside the laboratory setting. The Stage~6 monitoring layer therefore relies on proxy signals derived entirely from the model's output distribution and the current batch statistics.

Confidence is measured as the maximum softmax probability assigned to the predicted class for each window, averaged across all windows in a batch. Softmax confidence is easy to compute and highly interpretable, but it carries a well-documented limitation: modern deep neural networks are systematically overconfident, meaning that high softmax probability does not reliably indicate high prediction accuracy \cite{guo2017calibration}. Blasiok et al.\ demonstrate theoretically that two models can achieve identical expected calibration error while behaving very differently under distribution shift, implying that calibration metrics alone do not guarantee operational reliability \cite{blasiok2024calibration}. In this thesis, confidence is used as an early-warning signal rather than as a definitive quality indicator: a sustained drop in mean confidence is treated as a risk indicator that should be combined with other signals before a governance decision is made.

The defended runtime records lightweight uncertainty proxies from the softmax output, especially entropy and the top-two probability margin. Monte Carlo Dropout \cite{gal2016dropout} remains relevant as a research reference for epistemic uncertainty, but it is not required for the final Stage~6 runtime claim. The important operational point is narrower: a model can be overconfident under shift, so confidence is interpreted together with entropy, margin, temporal behavior, dominant-class share, and drift evidence rather than as a standalone correctness signal \cite{guo2017calibration,akbari2020personalizing}.

The defended runtime drift monitor uses a lightweight z-score mean-shift comparison against the raw physical monitoring baseline. This choice keeps the operational path simple, auditable, and aligned with the final raw-unit production input contract. If the raw baseline is missing or a baseline feature-space mismatch is detected, Stage~6 routes the condition to review instead of fabricating a critical drift score from incompatible spaces. The monitor should therefore be read as a production-oriented risk indicator rather than as a full multivariate distribution test.

Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) was evaluated separately as a calibrated experimental drift-audit signal. MMD is a kernel two-sample statistic that compares whether two batches plausibly arise from the same distribution \cite{gretton2012kernel}. In this thesis it was computed over per-window summary features and thresholded from reference-vs-reference comparisons, rather than from a universal cutoff. This branch evidence is used to test whether a stronger batch-level distribution signal improves drift visibility and governance quality; it is not presented as the final defended runtime retraining trigger.

This choice also explains why MMD is used for the offline audit rather than replacing the runtime monitor with a Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) or Population Stability Index (PSI) style check. KS and PSI are useful lightweight marginal checks when each feature is interpreted separately, but they do not directly summarize multivariate window-level IMU representation shift. The MMD branch instead compares batches through a kernel statistic over the chosen window summaries, making it more suitable for an offline multivariate drift audit. In this thesis, that advantage is deliberately bounded: MMD is used to compare drift visibility, not to authorize model mutation by itself.

A fourth operational metric, dominant class share, measures the fraction of all windows in a batch assigned to the single most frequently predicted class. This metric is not standard in the MLOps tooling literature; it was introduced specifically in response to the collapse behavior observed in this HAR application. In this thesis it is interpreted empirically: very high values (for example, above 80\% in the replay studies) coincide with low class diversity and collapse-like behavior that confidence or drift alone may not surface if the model remains overconfident on the collapsed predictions.

\subsection{Justification for Multi-Signal Combination}

The experimental evidence in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} directly motivates multi-signal governance rather than any single signal in isolation. The trigger-policy evaluation demonstrates that single-signal triggering achieves a precision of 0.8406 and a false alarm rate of 0.1389, while the \texttt{two\_of\_three} voting policy achieves precision 0.9878 and false alarm rate 0.0069. This improvement is not incidental: no single signal captures all operationally relevant failure modes. Confidence alone can remain high even when the input distribution has shifted, because an overconfident model may assign high softmax values to incorrect predictions consistently. Drift alone does not indicate whether predictions are still usable, since distribution shift can occur without triggering significant prediction degradation if the model has learned a sufficiently robust representation. Uncertainty alone may be elevated for genuinely ambiguous activities that the model was trained to recognize, without any pathological out-of-distribution condition being present. Dominant-class share can reveal collapse that is invisible to confidence alone. The combination of these signals under a voting policy therefore provides coverage across a range of failure modes that no individual signal can cover alone \cite{amershi2019mlops,hewage2022mlops}.

\section{Necessity and Limitations of Proxy Monitoring}
\label{sec:proxy_limits}

The reliance on proxy monitoring signals rather than direct performance measurement is not an arbitrary design choice but a structural necessity imposed by the nature of wearable runtime use. Ground-truth activity labels are unavailable at inference time in realistic wearable sessions: the device produces raw sensor readings, and those readings must be classified by the model without access to annotations from an observer \cite{dhekane2024transfer,khattak2023mlhops}. The labeling process that produces the supervised training data is inherently retrospective --- it requires a human annotator to review a completed session, assign activity labels to time windows, and export the annotated file. This process is expensive, delayed, and for many users impossible to sustain continuously, particularly in mental-health-adjacent monitoring where low-burden sensing is itself part of the design objective \cite{abdalrazaq2023wearable,gomes2023mentalhealth,levine2020passivesensing,khattak2023mlhops}. Longitudinal wearable-health reviews describe the same structural problem: naturalistic sensor streams are continuous and behaviorally rich, but labels are sparse, retrospective, and costly to obtain at scale \cite{lago2024longitudinal}. Proxy monitoring is therefore a common operational approach in ML systems where real-time feedback is unavailable, as documented across MLOps literature \cite{kreuzberger2023mlops,paleyes2022challenges,hewage2022mlops}.

The limitation that must be acknowledged alongside this necessity is that proxy metrics detect operational risk rather than prediction correctness. The thesis's own negative result provides the most direct evidence for this distinction. The unit-fix plus abstain intervention evaluated across 26 production sessions introduced three simultaneous changes --- gravitational unit correction, stream-reuse guarding, and low-motion abstain routing --- that were each individually plausible as improvements. The intervention measurably changed routing behavior, with more windows routed to the \texttt{REVIEW} state after the change. However, mean confidence remained unchanged at 0.8578 across both conditions. As Blasiok et al.\ demonstrate, a change in the distribution of prediction labels is not the same as a change in prediction quality when the model's calibration is held constant \cite{blasiok2024calibration}. The abstain mechanism successfully rerouted windows that the model would have classified with low motion signal, but it did not alter the model's underlying parameter state or improve its discrimination between the eleven activity classes. This finding is precisely the kind of operational nuance that a governed pipeline must be able to surface, and it would be invisible to a system that treats proxy metric changes as direct quality improvements.

Paleyes et al.\ document a related challenge at the systems level: operational reliability gaps in ML systems frequently stem from the absence of closed feedback loops between monitoring and model improvement, with monitoring systems that generate alerts but do not connect those alerts to a verified improvement pathway \cite{paleyes2022challenges}. The present architecture addresses this gap through the explicit governance gate at Stage~7: proxy signals inform the trigger decision, but the decision itself is conditional on multi-signal agreement, and any retraining action is gated by collapse detection and cooldown logic that prevents the system from acting on routing artifacts rather than genuine quality signals. The limitation is not eliminated, but it is managed through explicit policy rather than ignored.

\section{Threshold Calibration and Remaining Scope}
\label{sec:threshold_calibration_gap}

The final monitoring thresholds address a gap found during the pre-submission audit: the original warning defaults were too loose for unlabeled production-style sessions whose confidence values remained high even when behavior was operationally questionable. This issue deserves explicit treatment because it affects the sensitivity of every layer in the Stage~6 stack.

When a model is evaluated on data drawn from the same distribution it was trained on, softmax confidences cluster tightly around high values: the model has seen similar patterns, its parameters are tuned to this distribution, and output probabilities are correspondingly decisive. In the present system, the training set consists of labeled activity windows from six participants under controlled collection conditions, supplemented by synthetically generated data. Evaluation on this matched distribution yields mean confidence of 98.6\% and an uncertain-window rate of 0.18\% (Table~\ref{tab:labeled_baseline_reference}). Any threshold set relative to these statistics will reflect the narrow confidence variance of a model operating in-distribution.

Unlabeled production data does not share this property. Real wrist-worn sessions are collected outside the laboratory, with variable sensor placement, spontaneous transitions between activities, and backgrounds of ambient motion that the training distribution does not fully represent \cite{dhekane2024transfer,akbari2020personalizing,wang2024enhancing}. The model's softmax output is correspondingly less decisive: confidence varies more widely across windows, uncertain windows are more frequent, and the temporal-transition rate is elevated. As Guo et al.\ demonstrate, neural network confidence is systematically overestimated when the model encounters inputs outside its calibration range \cite{guo2017calibration}, and Blasiok et al.\ show that this overestimation is not reliably detectable from calibration metrics alone \cite{blasiok2024calibration}. The practical consequence for threshold-based monitoring is that overly low warning thresholds can remain silent on models that report 0.85--0.93 across evaluated sessions, even when those predictions are dominated by a single class and operationally unreliable.

The most extreme manifestation of this problem is \emph{class collapse without low confidence}: a model that confidently assigns every window to one activity class can pass confidence-based warning conditions while producing output that is diagnostically useless. The preprocessing ablation in Section~\ref{chap:experiments} documents exactly this pattern: gravity-removal-only configuration C raised hand-tapping dominance from 78.4\% to 96.7\% of windows with no corresponding drop in mean confidence. The fine-tuned pseudo-labeled model evaluated across 26 production-style sessions showed the same behavior: mean confidence remained at approximately 0.93 while the dominant-class share reached 99\% in several sessions. The earlier 0.60 confidence threshold provided no protection against this failure mode, which is why the final monitoring path adds a dominant-class tier and tightens the Stage~6 warning defaults.

Two threshold adjustments were therefore integrated into the defended Stage~6 monitoring path. First, dominant-class share is treated as a first-class monitoring signal: if a single activity class claims more than 85\% of all windows in a session, the session is marked as warning-level collapse risk, and at 95\% it enters the critical review band. Second, the Stage~6 warning defaults were tightened to 0.70 mean confidence, 20\% uncertain windows, 35\% transition rate, and 1.8 drift z-score. These values improve consistency between the code and the thesis argument, but they remain repository-level engineering thresholds \cite{shewhart1931economic,montgomery2009sqc}. They are intentionally conservative operating points: the confidence and uncertain-window thresholds surface low-decisiveness batches, while the dominant-class thresholds catch high-confidence class collapse that confidence alone can miss. They should not be read as clinical limits, statistical guarantees, or validated wearable-HAR constants. A larger held-out multi-participant longitudinal reference set would be required before they could be treated as statistically calibrated runtime limits \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}.

\section{Calibrated MMD Audit Branch and Runtime Decision}
\label{sec:mmd_audit_runtime_decision}

To test whether the lightweight runtime drift monitor should be replaced, a calibrated experimental branch introduced MMD as a batch-level drift-audit signal while keeping the retained model and input datasets fixed. The motivation was methodological: the current mean-shift monitor is simple and auditable, but the drift-ablation results showed limited sensitivity on degraded synthetic replay sets. MMD was therefore evaluated as a stronger distribution-level signal, not as a production scaler or automatic retraining trigger.

The MMD thresholds were derived from healthy reference data rather than assigned as universal constants. The calibration procedure repeatedly split the reference-window artifact into two reference subsets, computed MMD over per-window summary features, and used the empirical reference distribution to set warning and critical bands. In the final calibration, 500 reference-vs-reference comparisons with 384 windows per side produced a warning threshold of 0.00276403 at the 95th percentile and a critical candidate threshold of 0.00322704 at the 99th percentile. These thresholds are valid only for the reference artifact, feature representation, sample size, and preprocessing path used in this repository.

The branch experiments produced a useful but bounded finding. Compared with the current runtime drift monitor, MMD surfaced the degraded synthetic/replay datasets more clearly: the current monitor emitted 0 drift warnings across the four evaluated datasets, whereas the MMD branch emitted 3 of 4 warnings while keeping the reference dataset healthy. When MMD was inserted into the existing trigger evaluation, it produced 3 warning-level risk alerts with risk precision 1.0 and risk recall 1.0, but still produced 0 queue/retrain decisions. A final staged-policy redesign around healthy, warning, and critical MMD bands confirmed the same distinction: the hybrid staged policy improved non-mutating risk alerts, but queue/retrain recall remained 0.0 because the degraded datasets did not also cross the behavior-risk thresholds required for model mutation.

Accordingly, MMD was not promoted into the defended runtime governance path for this thesis version. The result strengthens rather than weakens the governance argument: better drift visibility does not automatically imply better retraining decisions. The defended implementation therefore remains the lightweight runtime monitoring path with explicit multi-signal governance, while calibrated MMD is retained as an experimental offline audit and future alerting direction.

\section{Chapter Summary}

This chapter formalized the post-inference governance layer used by the pipeline. Stage~6 observes confidence, uncertainty, temporal stability, dominant-class concentration, and lightweight drift-like behavior, while Stage~7 maps those signals to bounded actions under collapse and cooldown constraints. The MMD branch evidence shows that stronger drift visibility is possible, but it also shows why monitoring and model mutation must remain separate governance questions. The contribution is architectural: retraining remains deliberative rather than automatic.

It also established the metric semantics needed for Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}. Labeled metrics remain the basis for direct recognition claims, while proxy metrics are treated as operational risk indicators under unlabeled replay conditions. This distinction is the key guardrail for evidence-grounded retraining decisions.
