\chapter{Experiments and Evaluation}
\label{chap:experiments}

The previous chapters defined the data and model contract (Chapter~\ref{chap:data_model}), the implementation (Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}), and the monitoring and governance logic (Chapter~\ref{chap:monitoring}). This chapter reports the empirical evidence. Its purpose is not to claim a high final accuracy, but to measure what the proposed model actually does under three conditions of increasing difficulty: within-distribution validation, a blind external probe, and label-scarce monitoring. The central observation is a large and consistent gap between within-distribution learnability and blind-probe performance. This gap is reported openly because it is the empirical motivation for the runtime-contract, monitoring, and governance contribution of this thesis.

\section{Evaluation Setup and Evidence Boundary}
\label{sec:ch7_setup}

All results in this chapter are produced under the fixed runtime contract of the proposed model defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:data_model}: a six-channel IMU input of shape \((200,6)\), a 50~Hz sampling rate, a 200-sample window with a 100-sample stride, the eleven-class label order, and the train-only Garmin scaler. No result in this chapter changes any architecture, hyperparameter, augmentation, threshold, or acceptance decision after observing the held-out or blind data. The provenance of the numbers is also fixed: the model-building results were produced in the version-controlled build notebook that constructs and freezes the runtime contract, while the pipeline of Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} consumes that frozen contract for inference, evaluation, monitoring, and governance; Appendix~\ref{app:evidence} maps every reported number to its artifact.

\subsection{Evidence Boundary}
\label{sec:ch7_evidence_boundary}

The data sources are not interchangeable, and each can support only a specific kind of claim. Labeled Garmin users~1--6 support accuracy, macro-F1, and confusion analysis. The blind User~7 session supports a single descriptive external measurement, excluded from training, scaler fitting, hyperparameter selection, and model selection. The unlabeled session supports monitoring, confidence, drift, and trigger-policy evidence, but not direct accuracy claims, because ground-truth labels are unavailable at inference time. This boundary is enforced throughout the chapter so that no source is used beyond what it can prove.

\subsection{Fixed Runtime Contract and Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:ch7_protocols_setup}

The proposed model contains 967{,}499 parameters and is fine-tuned from an ADAMSense-pretrained base on Garmin users~1--6. The five-fold protocol is a window-level split with the scaler fitted on users~1--6 before splitting; it measures within-distribution learnability and is, by construction, optimistic relative to deployment on a new wearer. It uses the 3{,}755 contiguous-activity windows from Garmin users~1--6~\cite{hammerla2016deep,bulling2014tutorial,reiss2012pamap}.

Augmentation is applied only at training time and only to the training split of each fold. The three-fold augmentation (time-warping, magnitude scaling, and jitter) expands the training set; it is not an additional input feature and does not change the six-channel input contract from Chapter~\ref{chap:data_model}. Balanced class weights are used to counter class imbalance. The fixed random seed is recorded with each run for reproducibility.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:ch7_metrics}

Recognition is summarized with accuracy and macro-averaged F1. Accuracy over \(N\) windows with predicted labels \(\hat{y}_i\) and true labels \(y_i\) is
\[
\mathrm{Acc}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{y}_i=y_i\right].
\]
For each class \(k\), precision and recall are
\[
P_k=\frac{TP_k}{TP_k+FP_k},
\qquad
R_k=\frac{TP_k}{TP_k+FN_k},
\]
and the per-class F1 and macro-averaged F1 over \(K=11\) classes are
\[
F1_k=\frac{2P_kR_k}{P_k+R_k},
\qquad
\mathrm{MacroF1}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}F1_k.
\]
Macro-F1 is reported alongside accuracy because the eleven activity classes are imbalanced, and accuracy alone can hide minority-class collapse. Calibration is assessed separately in Section~\ref{sec:ch7_calibration}.

\section{Recognition Performance: Within-Distribution and Blind Probe}
\label{sec:ch7_recognition}

Two labeled evaluations were conducted. Under five-fold window-level cross-validation on Garmin users~1--6, the model reaches 93.61\% mean accuracy and 0.937 macro-F1. On the blind User~7 probe, evaluated exactly once after the model was frozen, accuracy falls to 36.96\% (macro-F1 0.350), a 57-point gap that is the central empirical finding of this chapter. The subsections below describe each result and what it can, and cannot, be taken to mean.
\FloatBarrier

\subsection{Within-Distribution Five-Fold Cross-Validation}
\label{sec:ch7_fivefold}

The per-fold breakdown is given in Table~\ref{tab:ch7_fivefold}. Fold-level variance is low: all five folds fall within one percentage point of the 93.61\% mean, which confirms the result is not driven by a single favorable split (Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}, Figure~\ref{fig:ch7_fivefold_stability}). This is in the same range as the published Garmin HAR benchmark of 87.0\%~$\pm$~1.2\%, obtained under a comparable five-fold protocol~\cite{oleh2025recognition}; the augmentation recipe and scaler policy differ between the two studies, so the comparison indicates consistency of magnitude rather than a like-for-like ranking. The result confirms the architecture and preprocessing contract are sound for the labeled training distribution.

This number is reported as a within-distribution measurement and not as a deployment estimate. Because the scaler is fitted on all six users before the windows are split, because windows from the same wearer appear in both training and validation folds, and because adjacent windows overlap by 50\% so that windows sharing half their raw samples can land in different folds, the protocol measures how well the model fits the known data rather than how well it transfers to a new person. The next subsection makes this distinction explicit.

\subsection{Blind User~7 External Probe}
\label{sec:ch7_user7}

User~7 is a self-recorded session excluded from every model-development decision (training, scaler fitting, hyperparameter selection, and model selection) and evaluated exactly once after the model was frozen. On its 989 windows, accuracy is 36.96\% (macro-F1 0.350), with a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval of \([33.9\%, 39.8\%]\). This is reported as a single descriptive external measurement, not a selection or validation metric.

The degradation is expected. The session is self-recorded and self-labeled, so watch placement, wrist orientation, and gesture execution style vary relative to the training population, on top of the natural cross-user distribution shift. The eleven activities here are predominantly fine-contact hand-to-face gestures (nail biting, smoking, hair pulling, ear rubbing, nape rubbing, hand scratching) whose wrist-sensor signatures differ mainly in arm angle, contact force, and timing, all of which are person-specific. A six-user training set cannot cover this variation. Activities with a wearer-stable IMU signature transfer better: hand tapping is recognized at 91\% even on User~7, while most fine-contact gestures fail. The cross-user gap, not model capacity, is the binding constraint.

What the result also exposes is that prediction confidence does not track accuracy. Mean confidence on the User~7 session is 0.858 despite accuracy of 36.96\%. A model that is wrong the majority of the time while reporting high confidence is precisely the behavior a threshold on softmax confidence would miss, and it is the direct motivation for the multi-signal monitoring design of Section~\ref{sec:ch7_calibration}. Comparison with prior work must be read carefully: the ADAMSense study reports up to 92.16\% on a single best-case held-out user using twelve-second windows and an additional magnitude feature~\cite{khan2021adamsense}, a different sensor setup and evaluation design, not directly comparable to a four-second six-channel blind probe on a different device. External cross-user validation is rarely reported in the wearable HAR literature, which is precisely the gap this probe addresses~\cite{shen2025passivesensing}.

\section{Calibration and the High-Confidence Failure Mode}
\label{sec:ch7_calibration}

The User~7 result exposes a calibration gap: prediction confidence and empirical correctness do not align. Modern neural networks are frequently poorly calibrated~\cite{guo2017calibration,blasiok2024calibration}, and in the label-scarce wearable setting this is not a minor issue but the dominant operational risk, because a shifted session can still produce high-confidence predictions. The dominant failure mode observed here is therefore high-confidence misprediction rather than low-confidence uncertainty.

Calibration is quantified with the expected calibration error, which compares accuracy and confidence across \(M\) confidence bins \(B_m\),
\[
\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|B_m|}{N}\left|\mathrm{acc}(B_m)-\mathrm{conf}(B_m)\right|,
\]
and visualized with a reliability diagram and confidence summaries in Figure~\ref{fig:ch7_reliability}. The reliability diagram shows the model sitting below the diagonal in the high-confidence bins, the signature of overconfidence. The bootstrap confidence interval reported for User~7 is a window-level bootstrap interval over the windows of a single session; it does not represent population-level uncertainty across users, and it should not be read as a generalization guarantee. Adjacent windows overlap by half, so the bootstrap samples are correlated and the interval is approximate; a block bootstrap over contiguous segments would yield a wider, more conservative interval.

This calibration gap is the direct justification for the multi-signal monitoring design of Chapter~\ref{chap:monitoring}. A single threshold on softmax confidence would pass a confident-but-wrong session silently, so the monitor combines confidence with temporal stability, dominant-class concentration, and input drift, and the trigger policy requires agreement across signals rather than trusting any one of them.

Three key observations follow from these signals. First, mean confidence (84.6\%) sits well above the 70\% warning threshold even though proxy accuracy is only 36.70\%, confirming that softmax magnitude is not a reliable proxy for correctness on this session. Second, the per-activity breakdown in panel~(b) shows the error is concentrated: hand tapping achieves high proxy accuracy while gestures with similar hand-to-face trajectories (nail biting, hair pulling, hand scratching) collapse. Third, panel~(c) shows that raw temporal instability is substantially reduced by smoothing, which means many prediction flips are isolated single-window errors rather than sustained confusions. The drift z-score of 1.18 indicates the input distribution is shifted relative to the training baseline but not to a critical degree.

\FloatBarrier

A per-class confusion matrix for User~7 (Figure~\ref{fig:ch7_confusion}) shows that the errors are structured rather than random. Hand tapping stands out as the reliably recognized activity, with 91\% of its windows correctly classified and a mean confidence of 0.99, reflecting a rhythmic motion pattern that is relatively distinctive and stable across wearers. Most other activities produce systematic confusions concentrated on a small set of wrong classes: hair pulling is predicted as knuckles cracking in 48\% of its windows; nail biting is predicted as smoking in 51\% of its windows; hand scratching goes predominantly to nape rubbing; and standing is confused with ear rubbing. These confusions are not random; they cluster among gestures with similar hand-to-face trajectories, confirming that the model is misled by the specific execution style of User~7 rather than being uniformly confused. A small set of classes therefore absorbs a disproportionate share of the predictions from many input segments. This is the dominant-class concentration pattern that the monitoring layer is designed to surface: when the output is heavily skewed toward one or two wrong classes, confidence stays high while accuracy collapses, and only the dominant-class signal can catch it.

A per-class ranking of the User~7 F1 (Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}, Figure~\ref{fig:ch7_user7_perclass}) confirms this pattern: a few activities, such as hand tapping, are recognized reliably, while several others receive an F1 close to zero. The macro-averaged F1 of 0.350 is therefore the average of a small number of well-recognized classes and many that the model fails on for this external user.

\section{User-7 Proxy Metrics: What the Pipeline Sees Without Labels}
\label{sec:ch7_proxy}

The blind probe result in Section~\ref{sec:ch7_user7} was obtained with ground-truth labels. In deployment, labels are not available. This section addresses a practical question: can the pipeline reach the same conclusions about session quality using only what it can observe at inference time, namely per-window confidence scores, the predicted class sequence, and input drift statistics?

To test this, the same User~7 recording was processed a second time as a schedule-aligned unlabeled run. The recording followed a scripted protocol in which one activity was performed per timed segment, so the known activity schedule provides proxy ground truth without requiring manual per-window annotation. Schedule-aligned accuracy is the fraction of windows whose predicted class matches the activity scheduled for that time interval. The labeled blind probe yields 36.96\% accuracy (macro-F1 0.350) at mean confidence 0.858, while the schedule-aligned unlabeled proxy yields 36.70\% accuracy (macro-F1 0.351) at mean confidence 0.846. The two conditions agree to within 0.3 percentage points on every metric, showing the monitoring signals track ground-truth session quality without requiring manual annotation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/Figure_7_8_user7_labeled_vs_unlabeled.png}
\caption[User-7 softmax confidence and low-confidence window rate]{Softmax confidence for the User-7 session. The two leftmost bars show mean confidence under the labeled blind probe (85.8\%) and the schedule-aligned unlabeled proxy (84.6\%), both well above the 70\% warning threshold. The rightmost bar shows that only 9.3\% of unlabeled windows fall below the 0.70 confidence level, confirming the overconfidence pattern: the model produces high softmax outputs even when its predictions are predominantly wrong.}
\label{fig:ch7_proxy_comparison}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

The agreement matters because the pipeline cannot rely on ground-truth labels in production. The confidence, temporal-transition, and dominant-class signals that Stage~6 computes from unlabeled windows carry the same diagnostic information as the labeled accuracy. Yet confidence alone is not sufficient: mean confidence is 0.846 on the unlabeled run while accuracy is only 36.70\%. The model is systematically overconfident, so a threshold on softmax confidence alone would pass this session without any alert. The monitoring design of Chapter~\ref{chap:monitoring} addresses this by combining confidence with temporal stability and dominant-class concentration, requiring agreement across signals before escalating.

A further indicator available without labels is the low-confidence window rate. On the User-7 unlabeled run, 9.3\% of windows have a softmax peak below 0.70. On the training distribution this rate is near zero. An elevated rate therefore signals, without any label, that the session lies outside the model's confident operating region. Combined with the elevated temporal transition rate (40.6\% raw), the pipeline has multiple label-free signals pointing to the same conclusion the labeled evaluation confirms.

Temporal smoothing offers a partial improvement. Passing per-window predictions through the majority-vote stage~\cite{bulling2014tutorial,lester2006practical} (soft vote over $k{=}3$ neighboring windows) raises schedule-aligned accuracy from 36.70\% to 39.43\% and macro-F1 from 0.351 to 0.373, and lowers the temporal transition rate from 40.6\% to 21.5\%, which falls below the 35\% warning threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:ch7_temporal_smoothing}). Mean confidence (0.846) and the drift z-score are unchanged by smoothing, since it only reorders predicted labels without touching the per-window softmax or input statistics.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Figure_7_9_temporal_smoothing.png}
\caption[Effect of majority-vote temporal smoothing on the User-7 session]{Majority-vote temporal smoothing on the User-7 session (989 windows). The transition rate falls from 40.6\% to 21.5\%, below the 35\% warning level, and accuracy and macro-F1 each gain roughly two to three points. Confidence and drift are unaffected.}
\label{fig:ch7_temporal_smoothing}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

The accuracy improvement is real but modest, and the reason is structural. Smoothing corrects isolated single-window prediction flips, but the dominant errors on User-7 are sustained confusions across consecutive windows: hair pulling is predicted as knuckles cracking across an entire segment, nail biting as smoking, and hand scratching as nape rubbing. A three-window majority vote cannot resolve a confusion that persists for dozens of consecutive windows. On the training users, where the base predictions are already strong, the same smoothing raises accuracy from 95.7\% to 97.9\% because the residual errors there are mostly isolated flips. The contrast between the two settings shows that smoothing is effective for jitter but not for systematic cross-user confusion, which requires additional training data or a personalization step.

\section{Monitoring and Trigger-Policy Evidence}
\label{sec:ch7_monitoring}

Section~\ref{sec:ch7_proxy} showed that the pipeline can track session quality without labels on a single reference session. This section extends that to scale: 25 recorded unlabeled sessions processed through Stages~1--7 under the same fixed runtime contract, and the trigger-policy and contract-sensitivity calibration that justifies the deployed defaults. On every defended evidence run the Stage~7 outcome is \texttt{action=review} with \texttt{should\_retrain=false}: the session is flagged for human review, and no model mutation is triggered. This is the intended behavior: a label-scarce monitoring signal indicates operational risk, not verified error, and does not on its own justify changing the deployed model. The preprocessing and threshold sweeps reported alongside these runs are treated as contract-sensitivity evidence, showing that monitoring severity responds to controlled changes in preprocessing while the model artifact remains fixed.

\subsection{Session-Level Monitoring Outcomes}
\label{sec:ch7_sessions25}

To test the monitoring and governance layer at scale, the recorded unlabeled sessions in the repository were processed through Stages~1--7 under the same fixed runtime contract. The analysis covers the 25 sessions that processed successfully under the standard per-sample ingestion path; two recordings with unusable layouts (one empty accelerometer file, one stored in a legacy Excel format) are excluded. Figures~\ref{fig:ch7_mon_confidence}--\ref{fig:ch7_mon_dominant} show each monitoring signal separately. Across the twenty-five sessions, prediction confidence stayed high: the average was 0.83 and no session fell below the 0.70 level, confirming that the overconfidence pattern from Section~\ref{sec:ch7_calibration} persists in deployment-style data. Temporal transition rates were frequently elevated (mean 40.3\%, 21 of 25 above the 35\% warning threshold). Distribution drift was mostly contained (mean z-score 1.18) with one session exceeding the critical level. Dominant-class share flagged two sessions, one reaching 99.7\%. The governance result is the central finding: the trigger policy routed \textbf{23 of the 25 sessions to review}, left one at monitor-only and one healthy, while authorizing automatic retraining on \textbf{none} of them.

This pattern is operationally important because the signals do not fail in the same direction. Confidence remains high and therefore contributes little to detection; transition rate surfaces instability in most sessions; drift identifies a smaller number of input-shift cases; and dominant-class share catches rare collapse. The review decision is therefore not a restatement of one chart, but the result of combining partially independent warnings under the no-auto-retrain governance rule.

One apparent tension is worth addressing. Temporal smoothing (Section~\ref{sec:ch7_proxy}) brings the mean transition rate from 40.2\% raw to 21.6\% smoothed across the 25 sessions, below the 35\% warning level on every session. If smoothing resolves the instability, why did 23 sessions trigger a review? The monitoring system always evaluates the raw prediction stream; smoothing is applied afterward in the serving path as a display aid for clinicians, not as input to the trigger policy. The review actions were based on the raw stream, where 21 of 25 sessions exceeded 35\% transition. This separation is deliberate: monitoring the raw stream captures the model's instability as it occurs, whereas monitoring the smoothed stream would suppress the signal the system is designed to surface. Appendix~\ref{app:walkthrough} illustrates these outcomes through the live dashboard.

\subsection{Trigger-Policy Calibration}
\label{sec:ch7_triggerpolicy}

The voting policy and cooldown used by Stage~7 were not chosen arbitrarily; they were selected in a development-time calibration study retained here because it quantifies why the governed policy is safer than naive alternatives. In the study, candidate trigger policies were replayed over the recorded monitoring history, and their triggers were scored against sixteen reference risk episodes identified in that history. Table~\ref{tab:ch7_triggerpolicy} reports the outcome. A single-signal policy, which fires whenever any one monitoring signal crosses its threshold, detects every episode but emits 251 triggers at a 13.9\% false-alarm rate. Requiring agreement of two of the three voting signals keeps episode recall at 100\% while cutting the false-alarm rate to 0.7\% and raising precision from 0.841 to 0.988. Adding the 24-hour cooldown of the deployed configuration reduces trigger volume further, to fourteen triggers at perfect precision, at the cost of not re-triggering on repeat episodes inside the cooldown window (episode recall 0.875). This trade is acceptable under the no-auto-retrain design because a triggered review remains open until a human closes it: suppressing a duplicate trigger does not suppress the underlying review. These numbers are calibration evidence from development, not a deployment benchmark; they are reported because they are the empirical justification for the two-of-three voting and cooldown defaults of Chapter~\ref{chap:monitoring}.

Windowing alone moves surrogate accuracy across an approximately seven-percentage-point range (66.6\% to 73.5\%) with the architecture, data, and training budget fixed, confirming that preprocessing choices are not cosmetic. The 50\% overlap lowers the prediction flip rate at every window size (for the 200-sample window, from 0.292 to 0.240 median), so the overlap choice directly shapes the temporal-stability signal that Stage~6 monitors. The deployed contract (200 samples at 50\% overlap) balances recognition quality, the four-second window duration, and temporal stability. Together with the trigger-policy calibration above, this sweep is the quantitative basis for the contract-sensitivity claim of Chapters~\ref{chap:architecture} and~\ref{chap:data_model}: window construction and preprocessing change accuracy, confidence, and stability even when everything else is fixed, which is precisely why they are bound into the runtime contract rather than left as free parameters.

\section{Synthesis and Implications}
\label{sec:ch7_synthesis}

The four sections of evidence in this chapter tell one consistent story. Within the labeled training distribution the model reaches 93.61\% five-fold accuracy, confirming the architecture and preprocessing contract are technically sound. On a blind external session evaluated once after the model was frozen, accuracy falls to 36.96\% while mean confidence remains 0.858: the model is wrong most of the time but does not know it. The proxy evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:ch7_proxy} then shows the pipeline can detect this without labels: schedule-aligned accuracy tracks the labeled result to within 0.3 percentage points, and the transition rate, low-confidence window rate, and dominant-class share all point to the same degraded session quality the labels confirm. Temporal smoothing narrows the gap modestly (to 39.43\%) and resolves single-window jitter, but cannot fix the sustained cross-user confusions that drive the bulk of the error. At scale, 23 of 25 unlabeled sessions are correctly routed to review, with no false retrain authorization.

The implication is not that the model is inadequate. Within-distribution accuracy is an insufficient basis for trusting a wearable HAR model in deployment. A model can match a published benchmark on the training population and still be systematically wrong and overconfident on a new wearer. The pipeline is built for this condition: the runtime contract keeps preprocessing and model coupled to prevent silent mismatch, the multi-signal monitor surfaces risk without labels, and the governance boundary routes that risk to human review rather than automatic mutation. The trigger-policy calibration shows two-of-three voting raises precision from 0.841 to 0.988 at full episode recall; the windowing ablation shows preprocessing choices alone move recognition and stability by several points. The experimental result is therefore the governance architecture, not a high final accuracy.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:ch7_summary}

This chapter reported the empirical evidence across three conditions. Five-fold cross-validation on the six labeled users confirms the model learns the training distribution and is consistent in magnitude with the published benchmark~\cite{oleh2025recognition}. The blind User~7 probe exposes a 57-point accuracy drop accompanied by high overconfidence, and the proxy evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:ch7_proxy} shows the pipeline detects this degradation without labels: schedule-aligned accuracy, low-confidence window rate, and temporal transition rate all flag the same session the labeled probe confirms is poor. Temporal smoothing partially recovers stability but not the underlying cross-user confusion. At scale, 23 of 25 unlabeled sessions are routed to human review with no automatic retraining, the trigger-policy calibration establishes the two-of-three voting and cooldown defaults, and the windowing ablation grounds the contract-sensitivity argument. Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion_conclusion} interprets these findings against the research questions and states the limitations and future directions.
