\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:background}

This chapter is not intended as a glossary of general AI concepts. Its purpose is to use prior work to justify the thesis boundary, motivate the design choices defended later, and show where the gap lies between existing wearable HAR research and MLOps research. The review is organized around five questions: what wearable sensing and HAR have already established; how far wearable literature supports mental-health-adjacent monitoring claims; why production conditions create failure modes beyond offline accuracy; which MLOps and monitoring ideas are relevant to an offline-first pipeline; and where this thesis makes a bounded contribution rather than a generic one. That framing matters because the thesis does not claim a new classifier family or a clinical anxiety-diagnosis system. It claims a governed operational lifecycle around a wearable HAR baseline.

\section{Wearable Sensing and HAR Foundations}

Wearable Human Activity Recognition (HAR) commonly relies on inertial measurement units, especially accelerometers and gyroscopes, because these sensors capture the linear and rotational components of motion that are most directly informative for body-worn behavior recognition \cite{lara2013survey,bulling2014tutorial}. For wrist-worn devices, accelerometers are sensitive to movement intensity, orientation relative to gravity, and repetitive motion patterns, while gyroscopes capture angular velocity and rotational transitions that help distinguish activities with similar gross acceleration but different wrist dynamics. This pairing is central to the present thesis because the target behaviors are expressed through short, repeated hand and wrist movements rather than through whole-body locomotion alone. At the same time, these activities present particular recognition challenges: actions such as hand tapping, hand scratching, hair pulling, and nail biting share gross wrist dynamics and differ chiefly in subtle timing and pressure cues, while also varying considerably across users due to individual differences in movement style, limb geometry, and wrist-worn device placement.

The foundational HAR literature established a pipeline that remains relevant to modern wearable systems: sensor streams are synchronized or resampled, segmented into fixed or sliding windows, and classified at the window level rather than at the single-sample level \cite{bao2004activity,lara2013survey,bulling2014tutorial}. These choices are not just implementation conveniences. Window length, overlap, sampling rate, and axis handling determine what temporal evidence is available to the model and therefore directly affect what later counts as train--serve consistency. Bao and Intille demonstrated early on that accelerometer-based activity recognition becomes tractable when motion is represented over short temporal segments rather than instantaneous samples \cite{bao2004activity}. Later surveys by Lara and Labrador and by Bulling et al.\ systematized this view and showed broad agreement that wearable HAR is fundamentally a multivariate time-series classification problem \cite{lara2013survey,bulling2014tutorial}. What these works generally assume, however, is a labeled evaluation setting in which sensing conditions are comparatively stable.

Deep learning extended this foundation by learning representations directly from raw or lightly processed IMU windows. Sequence models that combine convolutional feature extraction with recurrent temporal modeling are now a common pattern in wearable HAR because they preserve local motion cues while modeling longer temporal dependencies \cite{wang2019sensorhar,zhang2022wearablehar,khan2021adamsense,lyons2021improved}. LSTMs model long-range sequential dependencies in time-series data \cite{hochreiter1997lstm}, Bidirectional LSTMs extend this by processing sequences in both directions to capture past and future context \cite{schuster1997bilstm}, and 1D CNNs applied directly to raw time-series windows have been shown to be a strong baseline for time-series classification \cite{wang2017timeseries1dcnn,ordonez2016deep}. In that context, a 1D-CNN + BiLSTM baseline is not an arbitrary architectural choice for this thesis, but a defensible continuation of a well-established HAR modeling line.

The literature in this area therefore provides a strong methodological foundation for downstream behavioral monitoring. It shows that wrist-worn IMU streams can support fine-grained window-level classification and that sequence-aware models are appropriate when temporal structure matters. At the same time, most foundational HAR work is optimized for recognition performance under controlled experimental conditions. It says much less about how the same model should be operated once preprocessing drifts, labels disappear, or incoming batches no longer resemble the training distribution. That is where the focus of this thesis begins to diverge from standard HAR evaluation.

\section{Wearables for Stress, Anxiety, and Mental-State-Adjacent Monitoring}

Anxiety-related disorders represent a significant and growing area of public mental-health concern, yet objective behavioral monitoring outside clinical settings remains methodologically difficult. Clinical assessment typically relies on structured self-report instruments, validated rating scales, or direct observation---approaches that are episodic, retrospective, and subject to recall bias and social desirability effects \cite{levine2020passivesensing}. Body-focused repetitive behaviors (BFRBs)---including hair pulling, nail biting, skin picking, hand scratching, hand rubbing, and hand tapping---are one class of observable behavioral proxy that surfaces in contexts of anxiety or distress. These behaviors are not clinical diagnostic targets in this thesis, but motor behaviors that are tractable for automated recognition: they are expressed as short, repeated wrist and hand movements within the observability range of wrist-worn inertial sensors. The practical case for sensor-based recording is that accelerometers and gyroscopes produce objective, continuous, timestamped kinematic records without requiring user input or clinician availability, and are not subject to the retrospective recall or availability constraints that limit self-report and observational methods \cite{lago2024longitudinal}.

The literature on wearables for anxiety, stress, and related affective or behavioral monitoring is more heterogeneous than the general HAR literature, and that heterogeneity matters for scope. Abd-alrazaq et al.\ review wearable AI systems for anxiety detection, while Gomes et al.\ survey wearable sensors for broader mental-health monitoring; together they show a wide landscape of task formulations, sensing modalities, and validation strategies \cite{abdalrazaq2023wearable,gomes2023mentalhealth}. Some studies focus on physiological stress markers, some on affective-state inference, others on behavioral manifestations that may be associated with anxiety or distress, and still others on mobile passive sensing rather than body-worn motion recognition \cite{levine2020passivesensing}. This literature is useful because it establishes that wearable sensing is relevant to mental-health-adjacent monitoring, but it also makes clear that these tasks are not interchangeable. Inferring an internal anxiety state is not the same scientific problem as recognizing an observable anxiety-related behavior.

For the present thesis, the closer analogue is not direct anxiety prediction but recognition of behavioral proxies or anxiety-displaying activities from wrist motion. Khan et al.\ showed with ADAM-sense that activities such as nail biting, hair pulling, scratching, and tapping can be recognized from wrist-worn motion sensors using deep sequence models \cite{khan2021adamsense}. Subsequent domain-adjacent work has stressed the same general point from different angles: preprocessing and noise sensitivity can influence results materially, and robustness or resilience remains a central concern when moving beyond narrowly controlled demonstrations \cite{schapiro2023noise,alkurdi2025resilience}. A recent cross-activity and cross-population study by Sahu et al.\ reaches a related conclusion from physiological wearables: even when the task is simplified to anxiety detection rather than multi-class behavior recognition, generalization remains only modest across activities and participant groups \cite{sahu2025anxiety}. Longitudinal wearable-health reviews similarly emphasize that real-world monitoring produces temporally heterogeneous, sparsely labeled streams that are analytically different from short laboratory benchmarks \cite{lago2024longitudinal}. Across these studies, the shared contribution is not a settled claim that wearables can diagnose anxiety directly, but a more modest and defensible claim that wearable motion signals can support recognition of behaviors that are clinically or behaviorally relevant.

That distinction is important for academic rigor. This thesis does not diagnose mental health conditions. It treats anxiety-related repetitive activities as observable behavioral proxies and uses them to study the engineering problem of building a reproducible, monitored, offline-first MLOps pipeline for wearable sensor models. A wrist-worn IMU classifier that recognizes repetitive hand behaviors is a behavior-recognition system, not a validated clinical assessment instrument; it can surface patterns relevant to mental-health monitoring, but it cannot substitute for clinical evaluation. For that reason, the mental-health-adjacent literature functions in this thesis as application context and domain motivation, not as a claim of clinical generalizability.

The mini-synthesis here is that wearable sensing literature supports the relevance of the application domain, but strictly at the level of the recognition layer---detecting observable motor behaviors, not inferring internal mental states. It establishes that the target behaviors are real, recognizable from wrist motion, and subject to cross-user variability and small-signal recognition challenges that make them a genuinely difficult engineering problem. What it does not provide is an operational account of how a trained classifier should behave once the labeled training environment is left behind---when preprocessing assumptions drift, confidence signals arrive without ground-truth reference, and retraining decisions must be made under uncertainty. That boundary is where this thesis begins.

\section{Production Challenges Beyond Model Training}

The main distinction between this thesis and a standard HAR paper appears after training. When a wearable model moves from offline evaluation to production-style use, failure can arise even if the model architecture and class taxonomy remain unchanged. Transfer and shift literature frame this as a mismatch between conditions assumed during training and the distributions encountered at deployment \cite{dhekane2024transfer,chakma2023imuda}. For wrist-worn sensing specifically, Wang et al.\ demonstrate that sensor displacement can materially degrade recognition quality, and Akbari and Jafari show that personalization and uncertainty differ across users in ways that directly affect recognition behavior \cite{wang2024enhancing,akbari2020personalizing}. The implication is that offline accuracy is only one part of the deployment problem.

In wearable systems, these risks are amplified by preprocessing dependence. Units, gravity handling, calibration, resampling, axis conventions, and window construction are part of the effective model specification, not neutral preamble. Schapiro et al.\ show that added noise can materially degrade wearable anxiety-detection performance \cite{schapiro2023noise}, while the broader HAR literature repeatedly treats preprocessing and transfer assumptions as first-order sources of variation rather than as benign defaults \cite{bulling2014tutorial,dhekane2024transfer}. Train--serve parity therefore has to be understood at the full pipeline level. A model trained under one preprocessing regime may fail in service even when the raw hardware appears nominally unchanged.

A second production challenge is that labels often disappear at deployment time. Under unlabeled operation, the usual offline metrics such as accuracy, macro F1, or confusion matrices cannot be computed on arrival. Unsupervised domain-adaptation work in wearable HAR addresses part of this problem by adapting models toward target domains without labels \cite{chakma2023imuda,li2016adabn,wang2021tent}. Those methods are useful because they show that post-training adjustment is technically possible. However, they primarily answer how to modify the model or its batch statistics. They say much less about whether an incoming batch should be trusted before mutation, when retraining is justified, or how an operator should distinguish between suspicious data and legitimate novelty.

This gap makes post-inference monitoring necessary. Calibration literature shows that raw confidence cannot be treated naively as correctness \cite{guo2017calibration,blasiok2024calibration}. Bayesian uncertainty approximations such as Monte Carlo dropout provide complementary information about predictive instability under model uncertainty \cite{gal2016dropout}. Shift-aware HAR and adaptation studies provide a complementary view by evaluating whether current data differ materially from reference conditions \cite{dhekane2024transfer,chakma2023imuda}. These signals answer different questions, and none is sufficient alone: a model can be confidently wrong, poorly calibrated without obvious shift evidence, or shifted without immediate collapse in the output distribution. That is why this thesis later treats monitoring as a multi-signal governance layer rather than as a single threshold on softmax confidence.

A third challenge concerns mutation and traceability. Adaptation and continual-learning studies show why careless updates can damage previously learned behavior or conceal the cause of performance change \cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc,wang2021tent}. From a systems perspective, reproducible response to such risk requires versioned data, tracked experiments, and interpretable artifact lineage \cite{gardner2018enabling,bahaidarah2021reusable,munappy2019data}. Without this scaffolding, it becomes difficult to determine whether a changed outcome was caused by model weights, preprocessing, thresholds, or the incoming batch itself. That is a production problem even if the underlying classifier is technically sound.

The synthesis of this section defines the thesis boundary directly. This thesis addresses train--serve parity, preprocessing consistency, unlabeled monitoring, reproducible traceability, and governed mutation. It does not claim a universal solution to subject-wise generalization, a fully autonomous domain-adaptation system, or a clinically validated real-time intervention pipeline. Those limits are not weaknesses to hide; they are the conditions that keep the contribution aligned with the available evidence.

\section{MLOps and Monitoring Literature Relevant to This Thesis}

MLOps literature provides the lifecycle vocabulary that model-centric HAR papers often lack. Amershi et al.\ show through a software-engineering case study that failures in machine learning systems frequently arise at the interfaces between data, code, validation, deployment, and feedback rather than within the model alone \cite{amershi2019mlops}. Kreuzberger et al.\ generalize this into an architectural view of MLOps as a lifecycle that spans development, deployment, monitoring, and continuous improvement \cite{kreuzberger2023mlops}. Paleyes et al.\ make a related point from deployment case studies: the operational difficulty of machine learning usually lies in the surrounding system and decision process, not only in model training \cite{paleyes2022challenges}. For this thesis, those papers matter because they justify treating lifecycle design as part of the scientific contribution rather than as secondary implementation detail.

The most relevant MLOps elements here are experiment traceability, version control, testing discipline, and monitoring. Tool-support surveys and deployment studies consistently identify experiment tracking, data/model lineage, and validation pipelines as mechanisms for keeping machine learning lifecycles reproducible and inspectable \cite{hewage2022mlops,zaharia2018mlflow,amershi2019mlops}. In the context of this thesis, continuous integration is relevant because preprocessing, inference, and monitoring behavior need repeatable checks. Full continuous delivery is much less central, because the defended system is intentionally offline-first and does not claim automated cloud rollout. The goal is controlled reproducibility and auditable execution, not deployment maximalism.

The monitoring and governance side of MLOps is especially important. Sculley et al.\ describe how hidden technical debt accumulates when assumptions about data, code, and dependencies remain implicit \cite{sculley2015debt}. Practice-oriented deployment studies and tooling surveys likewise emphasize that operationalizing machine learning depends on explicit human processes, observability, and decision interfaces rather than on model quality alone \cite{paleyes2022challenges,hewage2022mlops}. In that sense, monitoring is not just an external dashboard function. It is part of the control logic that determines whether the current system state should be treated as healthy, warning-level, or severe enough to justify intervention. This is the sense in which monitoring is used throughout the present thesis.

Systematic mappings of machine learning on edge and wearable healthcare devices report a similar imbalance: the literature is rich in application-specific models and hardware choices, but comparatively weak in standardized operational structure, reproducibility, and lifecycle governance \cite{pereira2024edgewearables}. That observation matters here because it shows that the thesis is not merely borrowing generic MLOps language. It is addressing a gap that also appears when wearable-health systems are surveyed from the deployment side.

What generic MLOps sources usually do not provide is a wearable HAR case in which unlabeled IMU batches, preprocessing sensitivity, and conservative retraining rules are all central. They explain why lifecycle governance matters, but not how the signals and decisions should be structured for this specific problem family. The thesis contribution is therefore not to invent MLOps principles anew, but to translate them into a bounded, auditable, file-based pipeline for wearable behavior recognition.

The synthesis is that wearable HAR literature contributes the domain-specific sensing and model context, while MLOps literature contributes the lifecycle and governance framework. Neither literature alone fully covers the problem addressed here. The thesis sits in the overlap: a wearable HAR system whose main novelty lies in how it is operated, monitored, and updated under label-scarce production conditions.

The HAR-side studies do several things well. They establish viable sensing modalities, justify window-based time-series classification, and show that deep sequence models can recognize fine-grained activities from wearable data. Some later work also confronts robustness problems such as sensor displacement or unlabeled domain adaptation. What these papers usually do not include is a lifecycle view in which post-inference monitoring, artifact lineage, and retraining governance are treated as first-class system components. The dominant question remains whether the model can recognize the target behavior, not how the system should behave once labels vanish and operations begin.

The MLOps-side studies do the complementary part well. They explain why deployed ML needs versioning, observability, testing, and controlled promotion, and they expose the operational debt that accumulates when these interfaces are implicit. What they usually abstract away is the wearable HAR setting itself: IMU-specific train--serve mismatch, the ambiguity of mental-health-adjacent behavior labels, and the distinction between recognizing a behavior and inferring a clinical state. As a result, MLOps literature provides strong lifecycle principles but weak task-specific guidance for this application domain.

The literature gap is therefore not the absence of HAR models, nor the absence of generic MLOps principles. The gap is the absence of an explicitly governed, offline-first wearable HAR lifecycle in which preprocessing consistency, post-inference monitoring, trigger evaluation, retraining, and baseline updates are integrated yet operationally separated. This thesis occupies that gap with a deliberately bounded contribution. It does not claim a novel classifier, a universal domain-adaptation method, or clinical validity for anxiety inference. It claims that a wearable HAR pipeline for anxiety-related behaviors can be made auditable, reproducible, and governance-aware under label-scarce production conditions. Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} translates that gap into system design, Chapter~\ref{chap:data_model} defines the data and model assumptions that must remain stable, and Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} evaluates whether the resulting pipeline behaves coherently under monitored production-style use.
