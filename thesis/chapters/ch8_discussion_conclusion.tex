\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{chap:discussion_conclusion}

This chapter interprets the evidence from Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}, answers the research questions posed in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}, states the limitations and threats to validity, and outlines future work. The experimental results can be summarized in one sentence: the proposed model learns the known Garmin distribution well (93.61\% five-fold), degrades on a blind external probe (36.96\%), and remains systematically overconfident throughout (mean confidence 0.858 on User~7). The discussion below treats this gap not as a failure to be explained away, but as the empirical justification for the runtime-contract, monitoring, and governance contribution of the thesis.

\section{Findings and Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:ch8_findings_rqs}

The central finding is that within-distribution accuracy is an insufficient basis for trusting a wearable HAR model in a mental-health-adjacent setting. A model can match or exceed a published five-fold benchmark and still be unreliable and miscalibrated on a wearer it has not seen. The within-distribution result is legitimate and is reported as such; the unseen-user and blind-probe results are realistic deployment estimates and are reported without inflation. The distance between the two is the result. Table~\ref{tab:ch8_rq} answers the four research questions against this evidence.

For RQ1, the strongest evidence is the session-level decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:ch7_sessions25}: across twenty-five real unlabeled sessions the pipeline routed twenty-three to human review, caught one collapse-level session at a 99.7\% dominant share through the collapse guard, and queued zero automatic retrains while the confidence signal alone, the naive safeguard, would have flagged nothing. The answer is bounded in two ways: the signals indicate operational risk rather than verified error, and the thresholds were calibrated on the same session pool (Section~\ref{sec:threshold_scope}), so the result demonstrates the policy operating as configured rather than threshold generalization.

For RQ2, the windowing ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ch7_ablation} makes the contract claim quantitative: with architecture, data, and training budget fixed, window-size and overlap choices alone move surrogate accuracy across an approximately seven-percentage-point range and change the temporal flip rate that Stage~6 monitors, and the channel-statistics comparison of Chapter~\ref{chap:data_model} shows the input distributions the contract must hold together. Preprocessing and windowing are therefore semantic components of the model, not interchangeable defaults, which is exactly why the runtime contract binds them.

For RQ3, the governance design is supported both architecturally and empirically. The trigger-policy calibration (Section~\ref{sec:ch7_triggerpolicy}) shows that requiring two-of-three signal agreement raises trigger precision from 0.841 to 0.988 at full episode recall and cuts the false-alarm rate from 13.9\% to 0.7\%, and the cooldown reduces duplicate triggers without closing the underlying review. On the session-level evaluation the no-auto-retrain invariant held on all twenty-five sessions. Unrestricted automatic retraining would have acted on label-free, possibly collapsed predictions; the governed policy demonstrably routed the same risk to review instead.

For RQ4, the attribution rests on two observations that hold simultaneously: the channel statistics of Chapter~\ref{chap:data_model} show measurable distribution differences across sources; and the labeled and schedule-aligned User~7 evaluations agree to within 0.3 percentage points (Section~\ref{sec:ch7_sessions25}), which shows the pipeline measures consistently whether or not labels are present. Together these point to cross-user domain shift and calibration limits as the drivers of degradation, with a gross model--contract defect made unlikely (though not impossible) by the within-distribution result.

\section{Interpretation and Implications for Wearable Mental-Health-Adjacent MLOps}
\label{sec:ch8_interpretation}

The within-distribution and blind-probe gap has a clear interpretation. Window-level five-fold cross-validation measures whether the model can fit the labeled distribution; the blind User~7 probe measures whether it transfers to a genuinely unseen wearer. These are different questions, and the same model and data answer them very differently. The roughly 57-point gap indicates that the binding constraint is cross-user generalization rather than model capacity or preprocessing correctness, consistent with broader evidence that wearable HAR models degrade across users and devices~\cite{stisen2015smart,wang2024enhancing,dhekane2024transfer}. The calibration gap compounds this: the model is not merely wrong on unseen users, it is wrong while confident, which is the most dangerous behavior for a health-adjacent system because a confident-but-wrong session can pass a naive safeguard silently.

The implication for wearable mental-health-adjacent MLOps is that notebook-level accuracy cannot be the basis for promotion or trust. The system needs a runtime contract that keeps preprocessing and the model coupled, monitoring signals that surface operational risk when labels are unavailable, and a governance boundary that routes risk to human review rather than to automatic model mutation. The experimental evidence therefore validates the architecture of the thesis rather than a high final accuracy. The contribution is not a model that solves anxiety-related HAR for all users; it is a reproducible, monitored, and governed lifecycle that makes the model's limitations visible and keeps its outputs interpretable.

The operational machinery is as much a result as the model numbers. Every defended number maps to a versioned artifact through the run-scoped trail and the integrity-hashed runtime-contract package; the data, the contract, and the defended evidence run are restorable through DVC or the release bundle; a suite of 95 automated tests (dominated by monitoring, trigger, pipeline, and contract checks) runs in continuous integration on every push; the training and serving environments are containerized along the governance boundary; and on twenty-five real sessions the monitoring layer surfaced risk on twenty-three while permitting zero unauthorized mutations. In an area where models are routinely published without their operational surroundings, this auditable surrounding is the thesis's primary engineering claim.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:ch8_limitations}

The scope of these claims is bounded, and the architectural and mental-health boundaries set in Section~\ref{sec:architecture_scope_boundary} continue to apply. The following limitations are specific to the evidence in this thesis.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Small and narrow user population.} The labeled development set contains six Garmin users and one blind external probe. Cross-user estimates from six users carry high variance, and a single blind probe cannot establish population-level behavior.
\item \textbf{User~7 data quality and author-as-subject bias.} User~7 is self-recorded and self-labeled by the author, who knew the activity definitions and recording schedule. Both annotation timing uncertainty and a demand-characteristics risk apply: the performed movements may be cleaner or more canonical than spontaneous behavior. The procedural mitigations (exclusion from training, scaler fitting, and model selection; a single post-freeze evaluation) bound the statistical influence but cannot remove the behavioral one.
\item \textbf{Threshold--evaluation circularity.} As disclosed in Section~\ref{sec:threshold_scope}, the monitoring thresholds and trigger policy were calibrated on the same pool of recorded sessions used for the session-level evaluation. That evaluation therefore demonstrates the policy operating as configured rather than threshold generalization to unseen deployments.
\item \textbf{Retraining constraints.} The fine-tune-from-current retraining path and the candidate-versus-current governance gate were validated as a functional and reversible mechanism, not as a demonstrated accuracy improvement; no held-out retraining dataset was available. The protocol also requires several users to form user-held-out validation folds and cannot fine-tune on a single new wearer.
\item \textbf{Scope boundaries.} The system recognizes observable wrist-motion activities; it does not diagnose anxiety, stress, or any clinical state, and no clinical validation was performed. Results characterize this dataset and device, not wearable HAR in general. The defended runtime is offline-first and single-machine; on-device and real-time operation are out of scope. Monitoring and trigger thresholds are conservative defaults calibrated against this repository's distribution, not longitudinally validated decision limits.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

The most direct limitation of the current evidence is its scale. The labeled development set contains six wearers, and a single blind probe cannot establish population-level behavior. A larger longitudinal dataset with more users, recorded across realistic behavioral variation rather than a scripted protocol, would allow the monitoring thresholds to be calibrated against a representative session population rather than set as conservative defaults.

The cross-user degradation evidenced by the blind User~7 probe motivates principled per-user adaptation at inference time. Test-time adaptation and continual user-specific fine-tuning allow the model to update from a wearer's own activity stream rather than relying solely on cross-user generalization~\cite{akbari2020personalizing,jha2021continual}. Such personalization is a natural complement to the governed retraining path that Stages~8--10 already provide.

The model is also systematically overconfident on unseen users: mean softmax confidence 0.858 while macro-F1 is 0.350 on the blind probe. Post-hoc calibration methods such as temperature scaling~\cite{guo2017calibration} or stochastic inference via Monte~Carlo dropout~\cite{gal2016dropout} address this directly by aligning confidence scores with empirical accuracy, which would make the confidence monitoring signal more informative than a threshold against uncalibrated probabilities.

Several distribution-shift and domain-adaptation methods were evaluated during development but excluded from the defended runtime under the no-auto-retrain policy. These include lightweight test-time adaptation strategies~\cite{wang2021tent} and transfer-learning approaches calibrated for wearable HAR~\cite{dhekane2024transfer,chakma2023imuda}. Reconsidering these once a labeled validation path exists would allow drift signals to guide controlled model adaptation rather than route all risk to human review alone.

The served prediction stream is currently stabilized by a fixed majority-vote temporal smoother, which reduces the temporal transition rate from 40.6\% to 21.5\% without labels (Section~\ref{sec:ch7_proxy}). Replacing this heuristic post-filter with a learned sequence model such as a hidden Markov model or a conditional random field~\cite{lester2006practical} is the principled refinement: such a model captures the transition structure of real behavioral sequences rather than applying a uniform vote window uniformly across all activities.

The most direct path toward a deployed online service requires no architectural change. The inference service is already containerized (\texttt{docker/Dockerfile.inference}: FastAPI served by Uvicorn) and exposes predictions over HTTP, and the runtime-contract package is mounted as a versioned volume whose integrity hash the contract validator checks on every startup. Deploying this to Kubernetes amounts to pushing the inference image to a container registry, provisioning the contract volume as a PersistentVolumeClaim, and defining a Deployment backed by that image together with a Service that routes HTTP traffic to it. This blueprint is cloud-agnostic: the same Deployment, Service, and PersistentVolumeClaim manifests run without code modification on any managed Kubernetes offering, including Amazon Elastic Kubernetes Service (EKS), Google Kubernetes Engine (GKE), or Azure Kubernetes Service (AKS), and a cloud-hosted MLflow Tracking Server or model registry on any of these platforms could replace the local DVC artifact store as the lineage backend for the runtime-contract volume. Horizontal Pod Autoscaling would then scale inference replicas with request load, while the monitoring loop could run as a sidecar container or a periodic CronJob consuming the prediction logs. Governed retraining under this architecture maps naturally to a Kubernetes Job: the trigger policy submits the job when signal agreement and the cooldown gate are satisfied, and the existing review gate still requires a human decision before the candidate model updates the contract volume. Stages~8--10 already provide the governance-gated skeleton that a continuous-delivery loop for model updates would formalize; the engineering step that separates the offline-first prototype from a production-grade service is exposing that skeleton to a scheduler and a container registry.

Before any of these extensions could support mental-health-adjacent interpretation, clinical validation and an explicit human-review workflow would be required. Longitudinal calibration of monitoring thresholds against real clinician assessments, rather than surrogate agreement with a scripted recording protocol, is the minimum before the outputs could be considered responsible outside a research context. These directions are revisited from the scope boundary established in Section~\ref{sec:architecture_scope_boundary}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:ch8_conclusion}

This thesis developed an offline-first MLOps pipeline for wearable-sensor-based, mental-health-adjacent behavioral-proxy recognition, built around a runtime contract, post-inference monitoring, and a governance boundary that separates risk observation from model mutation. The empirical evaluation of the proposed model showed strong within-distribution learnability (93.61\% five-fold), degraded performance on a blind external probe (36.96\%), and systematic overconfidence (mean confidence 0.858). The thesis does not claim a universally robust anxiety-related HAR model. Its contribution is to show that a wearable HAR model needs an explicit runtime contract, monitoring, artifact lineage, and governance before its outputs can be responsibly interpreted in a mental-health-adjacent setting, and to demonstrate a reproducible pipeline where every monitoring decision traces to a stored artifact and every retraining action requires human approval.
