Here the choices are discussed.

From the research papers, MMD and Z-score play different roles, so they are not really competitors.

MMD (Maximum Mean Discrepancy): Gretton et al. define MMD 
as a two-sample statistical test that asks whether two sets of samples
 come from the same distribution. 
It compares whole distributions, not single values, and later work shows its threshold is usually set by a test procedure such as permutations or bootstrap, rather than by one universal cutoff number. Research has also extended MMD to functional data, which is relevant when data behave like time-series curves rather than isolated points.

Z-score: the research literature treats Z-score mainly as normalization or as a simple outlier baseline, not as a full multivariate batch-drift test. Time-series papers describe z-normalization as a standard preprocessing step, often applied per series or per dimension, but they also warn that it can sometimes remove useful raw amplitude information and is often used by default without strong evidence that it is best for every task. In outlier-detection studies, Z-score is usually one of the simpler baseline methods that stronger methods often outperform.

So, side by side:

MMD: distribution-level, batch-level, multivariate, drift-oriented.
Z-score: value-level or feature-level, mainly preprocessing or simple outlier scoring.

For your wearable HAR pipeline, the papers suggest this practical split:
use Z-score for normalization, and use MMD for drift detection. There is no official universal MMD range that means drift for every dataset; the threshold should be calibrated from your own reference data with a statistical test procedure
